The two-bit term format

BLB keeps the usual 0/1 encoding at its inputs and outputs. Inside a stored term, a variable can be absent, positive, negated, or signed. Each term has a separate integer coefficient. In the tables below, A is the arithmetic value 0 or 1 of the input, and X abbreviates the archive’s xA.

The sign of X depends on the input. A stored term is zero if any ordinary literal is false. Otherwise its value is the coefficient times the product of the signed factors. Two terms with the same masks are collected by adding their coefficients and dropping any zero coefficient.

For example, A!B!C retains A⁢(1−B)⁢(1−C) as one term. That is a count in this factored representation. Expanding into the article’s ordinary monomials gives A−A⁢B−A⁢C+A⁢B⁢C.

For a product of two whole terms, apply the table independently at every input position. Each ¬A⁢X case contributes a factor of −1, so an odd number of such positions reverses the coefficient sign. Different variables retain their respective factors. EBHashDictionary then combines equal output masks.

The enclosing signal formulas stay in the original 0/1 system. For signals p and q, the code uses 1−p, p⁢q, p+q−p⁢q, and p+q−2⁢p⁢q for NOT, AND, OR, and XOR respectively.

All four families of compaction rules

Rule 1 matches terms with coefficients of the same sign and one differing factor on the same variable. Rule 2 matches opposite signs with one extra variable on one term. Rule 4 matches opposite signs and differing positive and negative literals on the same variable. Rule 3’s branch is empty. In particular, the corrected identity X⁢R+R=2⁢A⁢R could combine a pair into one term.

For unequal coefficients, take a portion of equal magnitude from each coefficient and apply the rule to that portion. Keep the unmatched remainder. Rule 2’s signed case doubles the new coefficient after the portion is selected. The Rule 4 comments give these weighted cases, each in both input orders.

The engine applies these rules greedily as terms arrive. Two lists can evaluate to the same function and finish with different masks or term counts. The count is a property of a particular compacted representation.

Expansion, substitution, and back-transformation

The archive uses two different expansions. ExpandxA replaces one signed factor with two terms at a time. Expand then fills in a missing variable with the sum of its positive and negative literal. The intended identities are

X⁢R=2⁢A⁢R−R,R=A⁢R+¬A⁢R.

The second identity splits a partial assignment into two. Repeating it gives terms with positive or negative literals for every variable used by the expression. To get the ordinary reduced polynomial required by the general back-transformation, expand !A as 1−A and xA as 2A−1, distribute, reduce powers, and collect by the ordinary variable masks.

Symbolic substitution of a signal p for A uses p for A, 1−p for !A, and 2p−1 for xA. Multiply the replacement factors and preserve the original term coefficient.

Why XOR can stay compact

Writing Xk=2Ak−1, parity of the first k inputs satisfies a simple recurrence.

Pk=Pk−1⊕Ak=Ak−Xk⁢Pk−1.

Start with P0=0. Each step adds one term and multiplies all the earlier terms by the new signed factor. This constructs n mixed terms with integer coefficients. In particular, an 11-input parity has a direct 11-term representation. A pure signed encoding can write its signed output as a single product, with the sign determined by the number of inputs.

These compact forms can be useful for XOR-heavy subexpressions. A dense transform still allocates its full array, so fewer stored terms matter most when a sparse or factored path can use them directly. For dense ordinary 0/1 polynomials the fast method is the subset zeta and Möbius transforms. FWHT handles a pure signed basis. Mixed terms require evaluation or a basis conversion before either dense coefficient transform can be applied.

What the two projects contain

LinearBoolean3 is the earlier expanded 0/1 implementation. LinearBooleanBase.cs stores a 32-bit variable mask and a signed integer coefficient per term. It multiplies masks by OR and accumulates coefficients in a dictionary. Its TransformBackToBoolean sorts by number of variables, extracts the first term’s row, and removes it by AND with the inverse. The archived AndAction3 constructs and checks all 256 three-input functions. The source also includes Golomb-ruler constraints, a small input/output search experiment, and separate numerical solving experiments.

BLB keeps the same gate formulas but adds the two-mask format, local compaction, and word-sized blocks of symbolic bits. It contains SHA-256 stage experiments and reference hashing, Keccak-f[1600] experiments, and another Golomb-ruler solver. The checked-in entry point selects KCKReverseSolverActionMoreDirectWay. These source paths document experiments with symbolic networks. Their existence does not establish a completed hash inversion or a measured speedup.

The older code provides reusable examples of mask-based accumulation, bit-block adders, truth-row checks, and OR-of-AND constraint recovery. A new multiplier can use the plain 0/1 representation as a reference. The mixed form can then be benchmarked on workloads where signed factors or early zero products actually keep the work small.

Verification and source locations

A local harness compiled the archived C# against the installed .NET runtime and called the selected methods directly. Both archived BLB rule suites passed. The multiplication check compared 4,096 ordered products of all possible factor masks on three variables, evaluating every product on all eight rows. The compaction check compared 9,216 ordered two-term inputs on two variables, including unequal positive and negative coefficients, over all four rows. The original LinearBoolean3 back-transformation recovered the correct rows for all 256 three-input functions.

The rule identities in the tables can also be checked without the code. Set R to 1 and evaluate both sides at A equal to 0 and 1. Multiplication by the common R then gives the identity for every assignment of the other variables. This check distinguishes the valid formulas from the two incorrect comments in the unused Rule 3.
