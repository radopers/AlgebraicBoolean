Back-transformation proof audit

I found no counterexample to the back-transformation as stated, and no gap in the proof. I did find small counterexamples that show exactly why two of your rules matter.

I checked all 65,814 Boolean functions with up to four inputs using exact arithmetic. This time I tested every permitted next choice, including all ties, for a total of 104,879 distinct transitions. Every choice had coefficient +1, selected a true row, and removed exactly that row. Since each resulting polynomial is another state in the checked set, the test covers every possible tie-breaking sequence for those input sizes.

Two ways to break the algorithm are:

  • Choose any term with coefficient +1, ignoring degree. Take p=1−A−B+A⁢B. Its only true row is 00. The term A⁢B has coefficient +1, but choosing it would emit the false row 11. Your lowest-degree rule correctly selects the constant term and extracts 00.
  • Choose a term before reducing powers. Take p=A−A2. Every Boolean output is zero, yet the expression still shows a term +A. Selecting it would emit a false row. Applying your reduction rule first gives A−A=0, so the algorithm correctly stops.

For the proof audit, I looked for circular reasoning between “the chosen term identifies a true row” and “the reduced polynomial is determined by its rows.” I checked both claims by a separate algebraic argument.

Let mS be the polynomial for the complete row with ones exactly at the indices in S, and let xT be the monomial using the variables in T. We have the ordinary polynomial identity

∑S⊇T mS⁡(x) =xT ∏i∉T (xi+ (1−xi)) =xT.

The sum expands all ways of filling in the inputs outside T. Applying this identity to every monomial gives

p⁡(x)= ∑S p⁡(vS) mS⁡(x).

So a reduced Boolean-valued polynomial is exactly the sum of the complete terms for its true rows. If every row is false, that sum is zero.

Now consider the true rows with the fewest ones. Each of their complete terms starts with its own monomial with coefficient +1, followed only by higher-degree terms. Those first monomials are distinct, so they cannot cancel. This independently confirms your selection rule: the lowest-degree monomials identify precisely the remaining true rows with the fewest ones. The removal step then deletes one of those rows, leaving the same argument available for the next step.

That argument holds for any finite number of inputs. The counterexamples show that reduction and lowest-degree selection do real work in the theorem.
